# What Simultaneous Interpreting Taught Me About Learning English

My full-time job was in consulting. As part of my work, I collaborated with international appraisers who conducted software process appraisals to evaluate clients' software development processes and identify areas for improvement. I was also responsible for interpreting during these appraisal sessions.

The work often involved real-time, back-and-forth communication: the appraiser would speak, and I would interpret for the client; then the client would respond, and I would interpret their answer for the appraiser.

Sometimes, this would continue non-stop for an entire morning. It required intense concentration, quick thinking, and the ability to listen, understand, organize ideas, and speak almost simultaneously.

Recently, a colleague asked me again about how I learned English. Looking back at an article I had written in 2020, I realized there were still some practical lessons worth sharing.

Rather than repeating the usual language-learning advice, I want to focus on a simple principle that has shaped the way I use English:

**Focus on keywords.**

Whether listening, speaking, reading, writing, or translating between Chinese and English, identifying key words helps us process information and communicate more efficiently.

## 01 — Listening: Focus on Keywords

Connected speech is extremely common in spoken English.

Whether you are watching TV shows or talking to people, trying to catch every single sound can be exhausting. If you focus too much on individual details, you may lose track of the overall meaning.

I usually pay particular attention to three types of words:

- Nouns
- Verbs
- Adjectives

These words often carry the most important information in a sentence. Once you catch them, you can usually understand the general meaning, even if you miss a few smaller words.

**A useful resource:** Daily English Listening (每日英语听力), which offers a wide range of listening materials, including TV shows and talk shows.

## 02 — Speaking: Pronunciation Comes First

Chinese and English have very different sound systems. When learning English pronunciation, try not to rely on Chinese characters or sounds to imitate English words.

For example, many Chinese learners were taught to remember *morning* as "猫宁". It may seem like a fun shortcut, but it does not help develop accurate pronunciation.

If your English pronunciation sounds strongly influenced by Chinese, it may be worth going back to the basics and studying phonetic symbols.

A few things you can do:

1. Learn the phonetic symbols and understand how each sound is produced.
2. Listen carefully to the differences between vowels and consonants.
3. Practise in front of a mirror and observe your mouth shape.
4. Focus on developing correct pronunciation habits rather than simply memorizing individual words.

**Recommended resources:** Search for "Webster's Phonetic Symbols – Vowels" and "Webster's Phonetic Symbols – Consonants".

## 03 — Speaking: Communication Matters More Than Accent

The main purpose of speaking English is communication.

People speak English with different accents. Having an accent does not mean you cannot communicate effectively.

Instead of worrying too much about whether your accent sounds perfect, focus on making yourself understood.

When organizing a sentence, start with the nouns, verbs, and adjectives. Then work out how to put them together.

For example:

- "I wanna talk to you."
- "It says..."

*Talk* and *say* are both related to communication, but they are used in different contexts. Pay attention to how words are used in real conversations.

Speaking also has a rhythm. If you cannot think of a complete sentence immediately, start with the basic structure and add details afterwards.

**Speak first, refine later.**

## 04 — Reading: Understand the Message

The keyword approach works just as well when reading English.

Start by looking at the title, headings, and topic sentences. A quick scan can give you a general idea of the structure and main points.

Then focus on the important nouns, verbs, and adjectives in each paragraph.

The purpose of reading is to understand the message, not to translate every English word into Chinese.

I have always believed in this reading principle:

> Roughly right is better than accurately wrong.

It is often better to understand the overall message than to spend too much time struggling with individual words and lose sight of the bigger picture.

Of course, this does not mean grammar and details are unimportant. The amount of attention you give to each detail should depend on your reading purpose.

## 05 — Writing: Build a Collection of Useful Expressions

One of the things I write most often for work is emails.

Many parts of professional English emails follow relatively standard patterns, including greetings, opening sentences, and closing expressions.

Instead of starting from scratch every time, build a collection of useful sentence patterns and replace the key information according to the situation.

For example:

**Please find attached ...**

**I've forwarded ... to you.**

**If you have any questions, please don't hesitate to contact me.**

**I look forward to ...**

These expressions are useful in everyday professional communication. Once you become familiar with them, you can focus more on the actual information you need to communicate.

## 06 — Chinese to English: Identify Keywords First

During software process appraisal sessions, I often needed to interpret what clients said in Chinese into English.

In these situations, speed matters. If you try to translate every sentence completely in your head, check the grammar, consider the tense, and only then start speaking, you may struggle to keep up with the conversation.

My approach is to identify the key nouns and verbs first, then build a sentence around them.

In real-time communication, the first priority is to deliver the main message clearly.

For written Chinese-to-English translation, however, the challenge is often not just choosing the right vocabulary. It is also recognizing information that is implied or omitted in Chinese.

Chinese sentences often leave out subjects or objects. We can still understand them because the context fills in the missing information.

English, on the other hand, generally requires a clearer sentence structure.

If you are unsure whether a Chinese sentence is complete, try translating it into English. This exercise can help you identify information that may have been left unstated.

## 07 — English to Chinese: Read Your Translation Aloud

I mainly use English-to-Chinese translation when working with academic literature.

Since English and Chinese have different sentence structures and word orders, translating an English sentence word for word can easily produce unnatural Chinese.

My approach is to complete the initial translation first, then read it again from the perspective of a Chinese reader.

For long or complicated sentences, I recommend reading the Chinese translation aloud rather than silently reviewing it.

Try to put the original English sentence aside and read only your Chinese translation. If the Chinese sounds natural and flows smoothly, your translation is probably on the right track.

Translation is not simply about replacing English words with Chinese words. It is about expressing the original meaning naturally in the target language.

---

## Final Thoughts

Looking back, all these methods share one underlying idea:

**Don't get lost in the details of language. Focus on the message first, then refine your expression.**

When listening, identify the keywords. When speaking, communicate the main idea first. When reading, understand the overall message. When writing, build a collection of useful expressions. And when translating, adjust your approach according to the situation.

There is no shortcut to learning a language, but finding an approach that works for you can certainly make the journey more efficient.
